What is Software Architecture?

A practical, exam-oriented introduction to software architecture — with the additional perspective needed to grow toward Staff Engineer responsibilities.

Level: Foundation Focus: Definitions, goals, benefits Curriculum: CPSA-F 2025.1
On this page Learning objectives 1. What architecture means 2. More than diagrams 3. Goals & benefits 4. Architecture vs implementation 5. Trade-offs 6. Staff Engineer lens Exam traps Key terms Exercise Review questions Resources Progress

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain what software architecture is, why it matters, and how architecture differs from ordinary implementation decisions.

Official alignment This lesson primarily supports LG 01-01: Understand Definitions of Software Architecture and LG 01-02: Goals and Benefits of Software Architecture.

1. What does “software architecture” actually mean?

A useful working definition is:

Software architecture The fundamental structures of a software system, the elements that make up those structures, the relationships between them, and the important decisions and principles that guide their design and evolution.

This means architecture is not just about components. It also includes:

  • the major building blocks of a system,
  • their responsibilities,
  • their interfaces and dependencies,
  • cross-cutting concepts such as security, logging, persistence, and observability,
  • important architectural decisions and their consequences.
Software Architecture
├── Structures / Building Blocks
├── Relationships / Dependencies
├── Interfaces
├── Cross-cutting Concepts
├── Principles
└── Important Decisions + Consequences

2. Architecture is more than diagrams

A common misunderstanding is that architecture means producing boxes-and-arrows diagrams. Diagrams are useful, but they are only one representation of architecture.

Diagram-only thinking

“We have an API, three services, and a database.”

Architectural thinking

“Why are these boundaries here? What quality attributes do they support? What happens if one service fails? Which decisions are expensive to reverse?”

Exam distinction Architecture documentation can contain diagrams, but architecture itself includes structure, principles, interfaces, decisions, and consequences.

3. Goals and benefits of software architecture

Architecture helps a team build and evolve software deliberately instead of accumulating accidental structure.

  • Design: provides a coherent structure for implementation.
  • Communication: gives stakeholders a shared understanding of the system.
  • Quality: supports qualities such as maintainability, security, performance, availability, and scalability.
  • Risk management: exposes major technical risks and expensive decisions early.
  • Evolution: makes long-term change easier by establishing stable boundaries and principles.
  • Operations: influences deployment, monitoring, failure handling, and recovery.
Staff Engineer lens At Staff level, your value is often less about writing the most code and more about improving the quality of technical decisions across teams.

4. Architecture decision vs implementation detail

Not every technical decision is architectural. A practical heuristic is to ask how broad, long-lasting, and expensive to reverse the decision is.

Usually architectural

  • Monolith vs distributed services
  • Major data ownership boundaries
  • Communication style between subsystems
  • Security model
  • Deployment architecture
  • Core persistence strategy

Usually implementation detail

  • Variable naming
  • Small refactoring choices
  • Helper function structure
  • Local formatting decisions
  • Minor library usage inside one component

The boundary is not absolute. A decision can become architectural when it affects many parts of the system or is costly to change later.

5. Architecture is about trade-offs

There is no universally “best” architecture. Good architecture is appropriate for its context.

More availability  → often more infrastructure + cost
More security      → can reduce usability
More independence  → can increase operational complexity
More flexibility   → can increase design complexity
More consistency   → can increase coordination and latency

Architects therefore evaluate consequences instead of asking only whether a technology or pattern is “good.”

Exam trap Be suspicious of absolute statements such as “microservices are more scalable” or “event-driven systems are always more decoupled.” Architecture choices depend on requirements and constraints.

6. A Staff Engineer way to think about architecture

When someone proposes a solution, train yourself to ask questions like:

  • What problem are we solving?
  • Which quality attributes matter most?
  • What constraints are fixed?
  • What can fail?
  • Which dependencies are being introduced?
  • How expensive is this decision to reverse?
  • Can we defer the decision until we have more evidence?
Practical habit Instead of saying “I prefer X,” practice saying “I prefer X because it improves Y under constraint Z, but the trade-off is W.”

Common CPSA-F exam traps

  1. “Architecture is the same as documentation.” False. Documentation describes architecture.
  2. “Architecture only covers structure.” False. Principles, cross-cutting concepts, and important decisions also matter.
  3. “There is one best architecture for a given problem type.” False. Context and trade-offs matter.
  4. “Architecture decisions are only technical.” False. Organizational, regulatory, and business constraints can shape architecture.
  5. “Architectural decisions should never change.” False. Architecture evolves as requirements and environments change.

Key terms to remember

Software architectureFundamental structures, relationships, principles, and significant decisions of a software system.
Building blockA major architectural element with a clear responsibility.
InterfaceA contract through which architectural elements interact.
Cross-cutting concernA concern such as security, logging, or monitoring that affects many parts of the system.
Architectural decisionA significant decision that shapes the system and usually has broad or long-lasting consequences.
Trade-offA situation where improving one property may reduce another or introduce additional cost or complexity.

Practical exercise

You are designing an e-commerce platform with:

  • 1 million registered customers
  • 10,000 concurrent users at peak
  • catalog, orders, payments, and customer accounts
  • 99.9% availability
  • GDPR requirements

A developer proposes:

React
  ↓
Spring Boot Monolith
  ↓
PostgreSQL

Write down five architecture questions you would ask before accepting or rejecting the proposal.

Example answer
  • Which parts of the system have different scalability needs?
  • What availability expectations apply to checkout and payment?
  • What customer data is personal data, and how must it be protected?
  • What happens when the payment provider is temporarily unavailable?
  • How frequently do teams need to deploy or change different parts of the system independently?

Review questions

1. Is software architecture just a collection of diagrams?

No. Diagrams are representations. Architecture also includes structures, relationships, principles, cross-cutting concepts, and significant decisions.

2. Why are trade-offs central to architecture?

Because improving one quality often has costs or negative consequences for another quality, complexity, or budget.

3. What makes a decision “architectural”?

Its scope, long-term impact, cost of reversal, and influence on important qualities or multiple parts of the system.

4. Name three goals of software architecture.

Examples: improving system qualities, reducing risk, guiding design and implementation, supporting communication, and enabling long-term evolution.
