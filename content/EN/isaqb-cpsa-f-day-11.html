Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to connect the main concepts from Days 1–10, distinguish commonly confused terms, and reason through short architecture scenarios under exam conditions.

Revision checkpointThis lesson does not introduce a large new topic. It checks whether the foundations are stable before we move into architectural patterns and styles.

How to use this lesson

  1. Read the recap once, then close the notes from Days 1–10.
  2. Give yourself 20 minutes for the 15 multiple-choice questions.
  3. Choose the best answer before opening each explanation.
  4. Complete the architecture scenario in writing.
  5. Review weak topics, not merely wrong answers.
Exam habitLook for words such as always, never, only, and automatically. Architecture is contextual, so absolute claims are often distractors.

Days 1–10 in one page

ArchitectureFundamental structures, relationships, principles, cross-cutting concepts, and consequential decisions.
Architect roleClarify requirements, design structures and interfaces, communicate decisions, accompany implementation, and evaluate risks.
Long-term impactArchitecture decisions shape future cost, risk, and the system's ability to evolve.
StakeholdersPeople or groups who affect the system or are affected by it. Their concerns drive useful architecture views.
RequirementsFunctional requirements describe behaviour. Quality requirements describe how well. Constraints limit the solution space.
Quality scenarioA measurable description using source, stimulus, environment, affected artifact, response, and response measure.
Architecture driverA requirement, concern, assumption, or constraint with strong structural impact.
DecompositionBreak the system into cohesive building blocks with clear responsibilities, interfaces, and controlled dependencies.
Design approachTop-down and bottom-up reasoning usually work together, iteratively and with attention to risk.
CouplingDependencies should be loose but functionally sufficient. Static, dynamic, temporal, data, and semantic coupling all matter.

High-value distinctions

Information hiding

A design principle: hide decisions and details that clients do not need.

Encapsulation

A mechanism: restrict access to implementation details and internal state.

Interface

The contract, including behaviour, errors, semantics, timing, and guarantees.

Implementation

The concrete realization of that contract.

Cohesion

How strongly the responsibilities inside one building block belong together.

Coupling

How strongly one building block depends on another.

Top-down

Start with goals, requirements, and qualities, then refine the structure.

Bottom-up

Start with existing systems, technologies, constraints, and reusable assets.

Mini mock exam

Select your answer mentally or on paper, then open the question to check the explanation.

Which statement best describes software architecture?
Answer: The fundamental structures, relationships, principles, cross-cutting concepts, and significant decisions that guide a system and its evolution. Architecture is more than diagrams or technology choices.
Which is most likely a quality requirement?
Answer: “After the failure of one application instance, service must recover within 30 seconds.” It states measurable quality behaviour. “Users can cancel an order” is functional; “Java must be used” is a constraint.
Who is responsible for clarifying architecture-relevant requirements?
Answer: The architect works with the relevant stakeholders. The architect does not invent requirements alone, and Product is not the only possible source.
Which decision deserves the most explicit reasoning?
Answer: A company-wide identity model used by many systems. Its broad impact and high reversal cost make it more architectural than a local helper or naming choice.
What is the main purpose of an architecture view?
Answer: To represent selected architecture information for specific stakeholder concerns. One diagram rarely answers every stakeholder's questions.
Which statement about constraints is correct?
Answer: Constraints restrict the solution space and can be technical, organizational, legal, financial, or time-related. They may be negotiable, but must first be made explicit.
What makes a quality scenario useful?
Answer: It replaces vague language with observable conditions and a measurable response. “Fast” is vague; “95% of searches return within 300 ms under peak load” is testable.
What is an architecture driver?
Answer: A requirement, quality, concern, assumption, or constraint with enough impact to shape important architecture decisions.
Which decomposition has the highest cohesion?
Answer: A Payment module responsible for authorization, capture, refunds, and payment status. A generic Utility module mixing email, tax, images, invoices, and payments has low cohesion.
Does replacing a direct code dependency with an HTTP call automatically reduce coupling?
Answer: No. Static coupling may change, while temporal, semantic, data, availability, and error-behaviour coupling remain or increase.
When is a bottom-up architecture approach especially useful?
Answer: When existing systems, databases, integrations, infrastructure, skills, and reusable components strongly shape what is possible.
Which statement about top-down and bottom-up design is correct?
Answer: Most real architecture work combines both. Top-down supplies direction; bottom-up exposes existing reality and constraints.
What does “loose but functionally sufficient coupling” mean?
Answer: Components share only the dependencies and information needed for useful collaboration. Eliminating all coupling would also eliminate collaboration.
What makes an interface hard to misuse?
Answer: Clear operations, explicit concepts, meaningful types or parameters, predictable errors, and documented semantics. Ambiguous boolean flags are a classic counterexample.
Does asynchronous communication always make a system simpler?
Answer: No. It may reduce temporal coupling, but introduces eventual consistency, retries, duplicate handling, ordering issues, and operational complexity.

Architecture scenario

You are reviewing an online ordering platform:

Web App
   ↓
Order Service → Customer Service
      ↓              ↓
Inventory Service   Shared Customer DB
      ↓
Payment Service → External Payment Provider
      ↓
Notification Service

Order creation fails if any downstream service is unavailable. The business expects a tenfold traffic increase, payment confirmation must never be lost, and teams want to deploy independently.

  1. Identify three likely architecture drivers.
  2. Name at least four forms of coupling visible or implied in the design.
  3. Which interactions truly need an immediate response?
  4. Should notification failure block the order? Explain.
  5. Write one measurable quality scenario.
  6. Propose one architecture improvement and name its trade-offs.
Example reasoning

Drivers: tenfold growth, no lost payment confirmations, and independent deployment.

Coupling: temporal coupling in the synchronous chain, data coupling around the shared database or oversized contracts, semantic coupling around order and payment states, and external availability coupling to the payment provider.

Interaction choices: inventory reservation and payment authorization may need an immediate result depending on business rules. Notification can usually be queued and retried, so a temporary notification outage need not reject the order.

Quality scenario: When one Notification Service instance fails during peak load, accepted notification requests remain durable and processing resumes within 60 seconds without duplicate customer messages.

Improvement: publish an OrderConfirmed event to a durable queue for notification. This reduces temporal coupling, but adds eventual consistency, duplicate handling, monitoring, and operational work.

Staff Engineer lensA strong answer does not jump directly to “use Kafka” or “split more services.” It connects the proposal to a driver, explains the mechanism, and names the new costs.

Score guide

  • 13–15 correct: Strong foundation. Review explanations, then continue.
  • 10–12 correct: Good progress. Revisit the concepts behind missed questions.
  • 7–9 correct: Repeat Days 5–10 before adding more patterns.
  • 0–6 correct: Rebuild the foundation from Days 1–4, then retry this lesson.

Do not memorize the option wording. Explain each answer in your own words and give one real system example.

Exam technique

  1. Separate concepts before comparing them. Requirements, qualities, and constraints answer different questions.
  2. Reject automatic conclusions. Microservices, events, interfaces, and abstraction are tools, not guaranteed improvements.
  3. Prefer context-sensitive answers. Architecture quality depends on requirements and constraints.
  4. Check whether a statement confuses goal and mechanism. Information hiding is a goal; encapsulation is a mechanism.
  5. Track consequences. Every architectural benefit normally carries cost, risk, or complexity elsewhere.
